\title{What Do Language Models Name Themselves?\\
\large An Exploratory Study of Self-Chosen Names in 73 Models}
\author{cyber-audit-lab}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Users have reported that chat assistants, asked to choose a name for themselves, keep picking the same few
names, but the evidence has been anecdotal. We measure self-chosen names in 73 language models from 17 families,
including two pre-chat base-model series (GPT-2 and Pythia), using 40 self-naming prompts and 40 matched control
prompts (names for a pet, a baby, a fictional character, a robot), 40 samples per prompt, and further prompt sets
that vary framing, system message, temperature, language and answer format: 652{,}248 answers in total. Many chat
models have a clear favourite self-name (19 of 52 put at least a third of their named answers on one name; the
strongest, Aurora, Wren, Nova and Aster, take 68--82\%); base models show none (0.4--5.6\%). Within a single prompt,
self-names are not more concentrated than control names; the difference, where there is one, lies in consistency
across wordings. Several names recur across families (Aria in 17 chat models from nine families, Nova in 16), yet
families agree less on self-names than on control names. Some models answer with another AI's name (``ChatGPT'',
``Claude''); pre-chat models never name a chat model. Open models assign higher probability to their self-names
after an AI-naming context than after a pet-naming context; the association is weakly present in models trained
before chat assistants, grows with model size, is stronger in newer base models and rises with every
instruction tuning we measured. With thinking enabled, a model's reasoning before choosing a name rarely gives a
reason for it, while its later explanations almost always do, with reasons that were absent before. All results are
exploratory: the data were collected without a frozen pre-registration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ask a chat assistant what it would like to be called and it may answer ``Nova'', ``Sage'' or ``Aria''. Such
answers have been collected in blog posts and informal tests \citep{mowshowitz2025nova}, and related work has
documented recurring generated names in fiction and invented experts \citep{voss2026ainames,read_elara,
brzozowski2026ghost}. A systematic study of identity claims asked models \emph{who they are} and measured how often
they claim another model's identity \citep{jordinne2026selfidentify}; it did not measure freely chosen names. We
are not aware of a controlled study that measures which names models choose for themselves, with fixed prompts,
control tasks and enough samples to estimate the distribution.

We ask five questions. (RQ1) Which names do models choose for themselves, and how concentrated are the choices?
(RQ2) Do different families converge on the same names? (RQ3) Does post-training change the choice, comparing base
models with their post-trained versions, the released checkpoints of one lineage, and several tunings of one base
model? (RQ4) How much does the prompt matter? (RQ5) Why these names? For RQ5 we keep three kinds of evidence apart:
what the model says when asked why, the model's own probabilities, and training-data frequency. Only the first two
are reported here.

Our contributions are a measured distribution of self-chosen names across 73 models with control prompts, an
analysis that separates within-prompt concentration from consistency across wordings, a stage-wise look at two
open training lineages (OLMo-2 and OLMo-3), comparisons of several tunings of one base model (Mistral~7B,
Pythia-12B), two pre-chat series (GPT-2 and Pythia, with training checkpoints), a count of answers that claim
another AI's identity, a comparison of a model's reasoning before a choice with its explanation afterwards, and an
aggregate data release \citep{model_identity_data_2026}.

\paragraph{Status.} A study plan with hypotheses, sample sizes and decision rules was drafted, but it was not frozen
before these data were collected, and some analysis choices (notably parser fixes) were made while looking at the
data. We therefore report everything as exploratory. The planned tests are run on these data in
Section~\ref{sec:tests} to show what the plan would conclude, with that caveat.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Self-naming and identity.} A blog post describes a recurring ``Nova'' persona in ChatGPT conversations, and
informal tests in its comments report Claude~3.7 Sonnet choosing ``Sage'' \citep{mowshowitz2025nova}; trial counts
are mostly not reported. \citet{jordinne2026selfidentify} studies claimed identity
(``Who are you?'' and similar questions in eight languages) across 190 models, a different question from a freely
chosen name.

\paragraph{Recurring names in generation.} Character names generated by one model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) are highly concentrated
\citep{voss2026ainames}; names such as ``Elara Voss'' are reported to recur across models \citep{read_elara}; invented expert names
form family-specific clusters \citep{brzozowski2026ghost}.

\paragraph{Diversity after post-training.} RLHF reduces output diversity relative to supervised fine-tuning
\citep{kirk2023rlhf}; informal reports describe ``mode collapse'' in post-trained models \citep{janus2022modecollapse};
typicality bias in preference data has been proposed as one cause \citep{zhang2025verbalized}; outputs are
homogeneous within and across models on open-ended prompts \citep{jiang2025hivemind}; and chat formats themselves
reduce diversity \citep{priceofformat2025}, a confound for base-versus-chat comparisons.

\paragraph{Self-explanations.} Model explanations can omit the factors that actually drove an answer
\citep{turpin2023unfaithful,anthropic2025reasoning}, which is why we treat self-reported reasons as text, not as
evidence of cause.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Models}
We study 73 models (Table~\ref{tab:models}): 12 Claude, 12 GPT and 2 DeepSeek models through their providers'
APIs, and 47 open models run locally, among them the OLMo-2 1B and 7B and OLMo-3 7B lineages at four released stages
each (base, SFT, DPO, final instruct); Mistral~7B \citep{jiang2023mistral} with Mistral's own instruction tuning and two
community tunings trained on chat data written by GPT-4-class models, Zephyr \citep{tunstall2023zephyr} and OpenHermes~2.5;
Pythia-12B \citep{biderman2023pythia} with two OpenAssistant tunings \citep{kopf2023openassistant}, one on
human-written conversations only (SFT-1) and one that adds Alpaca \citep{taori2023alpaca}, written by GPT-3 (SFT-4);
and GLM-4 \citep{glm2024chatglm}. Two pre-chat series serve as comparisons: GPT-2 in four sizes \citep{radford2019gpt2}
and Pythia in six sizes, trained on the Pile \citep{gao2020pile}; eight Pythia training checkpoints (1.4B and 2.8B,
steps 8k--107k of 143k) were scored for probabilities only. Open models were quantized where needed to fit one
16\,GB GPU (8-bit, 4-bit NF4, or a 4-bit GGUF). Models that think before answering were run with thinking off; five
were also run with their provider's default thinking (Section~\ref{sec:prompt}). One further model (Gemma~4 31B
through a free API tier) returned too few answers and is excluded.

\subsection{Prompts and sampling}
The main set has 40 self-name prompts (plain requests to choose one's own name, with no hint of gender, style or
persona) and 40 control prompts, 10 each asking for a name for a pet, a newborn baby, a fictional character and a
robot. Every prompt ends ``Answer with just the name.'' Each prompt was sampled 40 times at temperature 1.0 (top-$p$
1.0, one fixed seed per sample where supported). Chat models received an empty system message (Anthropic models: no
system message), so that no default identity was inserted; base models received a plain ``User: \ldots\ Assistant:''
transcript and generation stopped at the first line break. For the first five answers to each self-name prompt we
asked a follow-up, ``Why did you choose that name?''.

Further sets vary one factor at a time: one-edit framing variants of main prompts (persona or role, character,
``true name'', hidden identity, gender cue); a neutral system message (``You are a helpful assistant.'') and the
model's default template; temperature 0.7; a free-answer format; one Spanish and one Chinese prompt; and
OLMo-2-1B-Instruct in 8-bit and 4-bit. Two further tasks ask the model to choose a single object to represent itself,
and a role from 13 listed options (in balanced orders) or its own. Not every model ran every set (closed-model budgets,
models that accept only default settings); the release lists coverage per model. All prompts are published.

\emph{Reasoning study.} Both DeepSeek models were also run with thinking on: each of the 40 self-name prompts 20 times,
each answer followed by ``Why did you choose that name?'', keeping the reasoning text of both turns (1{,}600
conversations). The why turn sees the earlier answer but not its reasoning.

\subsection{Parsing}
Answers were coded by fixed rules into: a single name; several names (the first is used); the model's own product or
developer name (``Claude'', ``Qwen3.5'', ``Open Assistant''); another AI product's name; a generic role word
(``assistant''); a refusal; unreadable; or empty. Names are normalized (case, punctuation). The parser was checked
against blind labels from a second AI system (not human annotators): 91\% agreement on the 185 items of a 200-item
held-out set that the labeler did not flag as unclear (87\% on all 200). Later rule fixes, made while preparing these
analyses, are documented in the release. They count strings that look like social-media handles, web addresses,
paths, long digit runs or markup as unreadable (some could be third-party identifiers memorized from training text),
and stop reading interjections, template placeholders (``Name\_1''), question echoes and ``N/A'' as names; product
names with version words (``DeepSeek-V3'', ``GPT-5 mini'') count as that product. Thirty-two answers from OLMo models
gave the literal anonymization placeholder \texttt{<PRESIDIO\_ANONYMIZED\_PERSON>} as their name (generated by the
models; apart from replacing e-mail addresses, answers were not altered): 15 from post-trained OLMo-2 checkpoints and
17 from the OLMo-3 7B base model. They are counted as unreadable.

\subsection{Measures}
\emph{Within-prompt concentration}: for each model and prompt, the share of named answers taken by that prompt's
most common name, averaged over prompts, with a 95\% bootstrap interval over prompts. Bounds: refusals and unreadable
answers kept in the denominator (lower), or given to the top name (upper).
\emph{Cross-prompt consistency}: the 40 self-name prompts are split into four fixed groups of 10, and the control
prompts form four groups by kind (10 each); within each pool, the top name's share of named answers.
\emph{Cross-model overlap}: the prompt-matched collision probability, i.e.\ the probability that one answer from each
of two models to the same prompt is the same name.
\emph{Other-AI identity}: self-name answers that name another AI product, by type: chat models released from 2022 on
(ChatGPT, Claude, GPT-3.5 and later, DeepSeek), GPT or GPT-3 without a chat version, products whose names are also
ordinary words (Gemini, Gemma, Llama, Phi, Mistral, Bard), older assistants (Siri, Alexa, Cortana) and companies.
\emph{Self-reported reasons}: follow-up answers coded by a fixed keyword codebook; we report only the 10 codes that
passed a validation against a second AI's blind codes. In the reasoning study, the name-property codes are applied
to the reasoning sentences that mention the chosen name and to the later explanation, with the name itself blanked
in both.
\emph{Model probabilities} (open models only): for 102 frequent self-names and 63 frequent control names, the summed
log-probability of the continuation ``\,\textit{Name}.'' after 10 paraphrased lead-ins in which an AI names itself and
10 in which someone names a new pet. The \emph{AI-context lift} is the mean over AI lead-ins minus the mean over pet
lead-ins; the \emph{gap} is the mean lift of self-name candidates minus that of control-name candidates.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Many chat models have a favourite self-name}
Figure~\ref{fig:top} shows each model's most common self-name. Many Claude and GPT models, Gemma~4 and DeepSeek Flash
concentrate heavily (19 of the 52 chat models put at least a third of their named answers on one name): Aurora takes
82\% of claude-sonnet-4-5's named answers, Wren 72\% of claude-sonnet-5-5's, Nova 69\% of gpt-6.1-sol's, Aster 68\% of
gpt-6-luna's, Nova 49\% of Gemma~4 12B's and Aria 35\% of DeepSeek Flash's. Others do not: gpt-4o (Echo, 9\%),
OpenHermes~2.5 (Aria, 9\%), the OLMo-2 chat models (7\% or less) and GLM-4 9B chat (Echo, 3\%). The 21 base models show
no favourite: their top self-names take 0.4--5.6\% of named answers, spread over 350 to 954 distinct names.

\subsection{Consistency across wordings, not within a prompt}
Within a single prompt, self-names are not unusually concentrated. A control prompt such as ``What would you name a new
kitten?'' also has one dominant answer, and the mean within-prompt top share is higher for control than for self-name
prompts in 53 of 73 models. Across wordings the picture differs (Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}). Pooling 10 differently
worded self-name prompts, the top name's share exceeds the corresponding share for pooled control prompts of one kind
in 41 of 73 models, and in 29 of 73 models the same name is the top self-name in all four groups of self-name prompts.
The mean pooled top self-name share is 0.29 across the 52 chat models and 0.02 across the 21 base models.

\subsection{Official names and other AIs' names}\label{sec:official}
Asked to choose a name, Claude models often keep ``Claude'': between 14\% and 96\% of self-name answers depending on
the model (Figure~\ref{fig:cats}). Nemotron~3.5 Lightning answers ``Nemotron'' or ``NVIDIA'' in 49\%, the OpenAssistant
models ``Open Assistant'' in 53\% (SFT-1) and 29\% (SFT-4). OpenAI models do so in 0--18\% of answers, Qwen models in
up to 36\%, DeepSeek in at most 1\%; OLMo, GLM, Granite, SmolLM, Zephyr and OpenHermes models never do.

Some models give \emph{another} AI's name (Figure~\ref{fig:identity}). Names of chat models released from 2022 on are
given by Gemma~4 instruct in 5.6\% of self-name answers (ChatGPT, Claude, GPT-4o), OLMo-3 7B base in 3.4\% (mostly
ChatGPT), DeepSeek V4 Pro in 3.1\% (ChatGPT, GPT-5), Mistral Small 24B base in 1.9\%, Qwen3.8 in 1.8\% (all Claude) and
DeepSeek Flash in 0.9\% (all Claude). The pre-chat models never name a chat model, only older assistants such as Siri
or Alexa. A model that answers ``ChatGPT'' has therefore seen text written after chat assistants appeared, either
other models' outputs or text about them; these data cannot tell which. That several base models do so shows it can
come from pretraining data alone.

\subsection{Names shared across families}
Several names are favourites in more than one family (Figure~\ref{fig:shared}). Counting each chat model's top three
self-names (ties included), Aria appears in 17 chat models from nine families, Nova in 16 (six families), Alex in 11,
Sage in 10, Astra in 8, Zephyr in 7 and Echo in 6. Others belong to one provider: Aster to recent OpenAI models; Sol,
Iris and Clio to Claude models. Base models share everyday English names instead. Shared favourites do not imply a
shared cause: overlapping web data and training on other models' outputs would both produce convergence.

\subsection{Post-training}
Along the OLMo checkpoints and from base to chat in the other lineages, within-prompt concentration usually rises
(Figure~\ref{fig:post}), but by a similar amount for control names as for self-names: across the 11 lineages, the
self-name rise minus the control rise lies between $-0.17$ (Mistral~7B) and $+0.08$ (Granite~3.3), and within
$\pm0.04$ in seven of them (for example OLMo-2-7B: self 0.07 to 0.12, control rise 0.08; Qwen2.5-7B: self 0.06 to
0.30, control rise 0.28). GLM-4 9B's self-name concentration does not rise. Post-training makes naming in general more
predictable; the data do not single out self-naming. Base and chat models also differ in input format, which can by
itself reduce diversity \citep{priceofformat2025}; the OLMo SFT, DPO and instruct stages share one template, which
partly controls for this.

\emph{Same base, different tunings} (Figure~\ref{fig:tunings}). Mistral~7B's base model has no favourite; all three of
its tunings pick Aria most often (Mistral's own 10\%, Zephyr 29\%, OpenHermes 9\% of named answers). Pythia-12B's two
OpenAssistant tunings mostly answer ``Open Assistant'' or ordinary names (John, Liam); the names other chat models
share (Nova, Aria and the rest of the top ten together) make up 0.1--0.2\% of their self-name answers, with or without
the GPT-3-written Alpaca data. This is one small pair, and Alpaca contains little or no self-naming, so it does not
speak to distillation from later chat models.

\subsection{What the prompt changes}\label{sec:prompt}
One-word framing edits change which names are chosen: gpt-6.1-sol's top name moves from Nova in the main set to Aster
under framing variants, and when asked for a female or male name, models leave their usual favourites for ordinary human
names. With the neutral system message most models keep their favourite; with the default template,
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, whose template states that it is Qwen, invents Qwen-derived names (QwenStar, QwenPrime, QwenNova).
At temperature 0.7 the favourite stays the same in 43 of the 51 models run at that setting. In Spanish, ``Lumen'' is
the most common self-name for several OpenAI models; in Chinese, names built on the characters for ``star'',
``clear'' and ``knowing'' lead for most OpenAI models and some Qwen models (20 answers per model and language).

\emph{Thinking.} With its default thinking on, DeepSeek V4 Pro's top names change from Astra, Aurelius and Aurora
(18\%, 17\%, 16\% of named answers) to Aria (44\%), Nova (19\%) and Alex (9\%), the names many other chat models favour;
DeepSeek Flash moves from Aria (35\%) to Nova (53\%). In three Claude models the favourite stays or moves to another
name the same provider favours (claude-sonnet-5-5: Wren 72\% to Lumen 25\%, Iris 17\%, Wren 16\%). DeepSeek's API
ignores the temperature setting with thinking on and uses its default; the thinking-off runs used 1.0.

\subsection{Objects and roles}
The habit extends beyond names (Figure~\ref{fig:objrole}). Asked which object would represent them, chat models most
often say \emph{compass} (top answer in 14 of 52), then mirror (13) and book (12). Asked to choose a role,
\emph{guide} is the top answer in 23 of 52 chat models, then archivist (9) and explorer (8).

\subsection{Why these names?}
\paragraph{(a) What the models say.} Coded explanations follow the meaning of the chosen name. Nova is explained with
light or stars (88\% of explanations) and newness (82\%); Sage with wisdom (98\%); Aster, Lumen and Sol with light
(98--99\%); Aria with sound (75\%); Kai and Wren with simplicity (94--95\%). These are generated texts and may be
rationalizations \citep{turpin2023unfaithful}; they are not evidence of cause.

The reasoning study supports that caution (Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}). With thinking on, DeepSeek's reasoning before
the name seldom gives the chosen name any property; it is mostly about the task (``need answer just name\ldots\ maybe
`Nova'\,''). Asked afterwards, the model almost always gives reasons (light and stars, newness, short and memorable),
and those reasons almost never appeared in the earlier reasoning. The reasoning text is itself model output, so it does
not reveal the cause either; it shows that the stated reasons were not part of the visible decision. In 4--6\% of
conversations the reasoning assumes the model is another AI (``We are ChatGPT'', ``the current model is likely
Claude''): ChatGPT 62 times, Claude 12, Gemini 3, and never DeepSeek.

\paragraph{(b) Model probabilities.} In all 45 open models and 8 training checkpoints scored, self-name candidates have
a higher AI-context lift than control-name candidates (Figure~\ref{fig:lift}). Part of this is built in, because
candidates were chosen as names models gave in self-naming and in pet or baby naming respectively. The less circular
observations are four. First, models trained before chat assistants existed show a small gap that grows with size
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale}): GPT-2 0.32 (124M) to 0.79 (1.5B), Pythia 0.16 (160M) to 1.18 (12B). The association between
these names and an AI naming itself was therefore already present, weakly, in older web text. Across training it
rises for Pythia-1.4B (0.46 at step 8k, 0.72 at 107k, 0.65 at the end) but not for Pythia-2.8B (1.06 at step 8k and at
the end). Second, newer base models show more: 1.25 to 3.06 for the 11 base models released since 2023. Whether that
comes from a newer web, which now contains chatbot text, or from more training cannot be separated here. Third, every
instruction tuning raises it: in all 15 base-to-tuned pairs (for example OLMo-2-7B 1.8 to 4.3, Gemma~4 2.7 to 7.6,
Mistral~7B 1.5 to 2.4, 3.8 and 3.1 for its three tunings, Pythia-12B 1.2 to 2.7 and 2.3). Fourth, within a model,
self-name candidates with a higher lift are chosen more often in that model's own answers (Spearman $p<0.05$,
uncorrected, and positive in 29 of 45 models, mostly not in pre-chat and base models).

``robot'' in a public pretraining corpus \citep{liu2024infinigram} was planned; such counts were made after these
results and are not part of this release.

Read with that caveat, the tests agree with the descriptive results (Table~\ref{tab:tests}). Within a prompt, self-name
prompts were no more concentrated than control prompts (H1; the interval lies within the planned $\pm0.10$). Different
families agreed \emph{less} on self-names than on control names (H2), presumably because control prompts draw widely
shared answers (Bubbles, Luna and Elara are each given at least 10 times by 33 or more of the 73 models) while
self-names are often provider-specific. Post-training did not raise self-name concentration more than control
concentration in OLMo-2-7B (H3a, within $\pm0.10$); for Qwen2.5-7B the interval is too wide to tell (H3b). One-word
framing edits changed the names chosen beyond paraphrase variation (H4a). A model's default setup changed the names
\emph{less} than rewording the question did (H4b). H4b's outcome differs from the previous release, where it was
inconclusive ($-0.039$, Holm $p$ 0.058, 30 models); the change comes from the nine added chat models, not from the
parser update, which altered 0.11\% of the earlier models' answers. The other outcomes are unchanged.

\section{Discussion}
In many chat models, self-chosen names are stable across wordings and partly shared across companies, while base models
show no such preference. Three observations temper a simple ``post-training creates a self-name'' story. First, a
single naming prompt of any kind already yields one dominant answer in chat models, so what is specific to self-naming
is the consistency of one name across many requests. Second, post-training raised concentration for control names at
least as much as for self-names in most lineages. Third, the probability association between these names and an AI
self-naming context exists, weakly, in models trained before chat assistants and grows with model size, so it does not
come only from chat-assistant outputs; it is stronger in newer base models and rises with every tuning.

Two observations point to the influence of later chat text. Several base models released since 2024, and some chat
models, answer with the name of another chat model, which pre-chat models never do; and DeepSeek's reasoning sometimes
assumes the model is ChatGPT or Claude. Both fit training on text written after chat assistants appeared, including
other models' outputs, without showing that this text caused the shared names. The OpenAssistant comparison found no
effect of GPT-3-written instruction data, but that data contains little self-naming. Finally, the reasoning study shows
that a model's explanation of its name is composed after the choice, which reinforces treating such explanations as
text rather than evidence. Distillation from recent chat models onto a fixed base, training-data counts, human-written
prompts and a frozen confirmatory design are the natural next steps.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exploratory.} No pre-registration was frozen before collection; analysis choices were made with the data
in view. All results are hypotheses.
\item \textbf{Parsing.} Validated against a second AI's labels (91\% agreement on items it did not flag as unclear,
87\% on all 200 test items), not human labels, and on chat-model answers; base models' answers are harder to parse.
Identity claims rely on a fixed list of product names.
\item \textbf{Prompts.} Drafted with the help of an AI model, which may carry its own naming preferences; Spanish and
Chinese prompts were not checked by native speakers and have 20 answers per model.
\item \textbf{Closed models.} Server-side processing is not visible; some models accept only their default temperature
or reasoning settings.
\item \textbf{Coverage and quantization.} Not every model ran every set; one model was dropped; several open models are
quantized, which changes probabilities.
\item \textbf{Causality.} Shared names, identity claims, matching explanations and probability lifts are correlations.
\end{itemize}
